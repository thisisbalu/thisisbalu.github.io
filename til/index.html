    Cube to Parallelopiped in 3D

    Continued the topics I left off on yesterday. One concept that clicked for me was that the determinant of a matrix represents the scaling factor of a transformation. If the transformation reverses the orientation of the space, the determinant becomes negative.

    In 2D, a unit square is transformed into a parallelogram, and the determinant tells us how the area is scaled.

    In 3D, the determinant represents the scaling of volume. A cube transforms into a parallelepiped (a new term I learned today!), and the determinant gives the volume scaling factor.

    Another interesting insight is that when the determinant is 0, the transformation collapses the 3D volume into a lower-dimensional object, such as a plane, meaning there is no volume left :)

    Visualizing linear algebra

    I wanted to learn Dirac’s equation, but linear algebra comes first. Seeing it visually made the concepts click in a way the formulas never did.

    Three views of a vector:

    1. Physics: an arrow in space, defined only by its length and direction. It can sit anywhere as long as those two stay the same.

    2. Computer science: an ordered list of numbers. A house could be the 2D vector (square footage, price). Order matters, and dimension is the length of the list.

    3. Mathematics: anything that supports vector addition and scalar multiplication, regardless of how it’s represented.

    Linear transformations A transformation is a function that takes a vector in and gives a vector out. It’s linear if grid lines stay parallel and evenly spaced and the origin stays fixed.

    Matrices and multiplication A matrix records where the basis vectors land: each column is the landing spot of one basis vector. Matrix multiplication is composing transformations, applied right to left. In AB, B acts first, then A.

    Bosons and Fermions

    So I read a little about quantum computing and somehow ended up learning that every particle in the universe is either a boson or a fermion 😄

    Fermions (electrons, protons, neutrons) have half integer spin and an antisymmetric wave function. Bosons (like photons) have integer spin and a symmetric wave function.

    One thing I found interesting: Helium 4 behaves as a boson because its total spin is 0.

    And then I suddenly remembered an episode from The Big Bang Theory 😄. Turns out those boson vs fermion jokes hit differently when you actually know what they are.

    RSA is hard because factoring is hard, Quantum computing changes that!

    Was reading about some quantum computing concepts today and stumbled upon Shor’s Algorithm and Grover’s Algorithm.

    I’ve always been curious about how quantum computing could threaten modern cryptography. Got to know that RSA’s security basically relies on the fact that multiplying two large primes is easy, but finding the original primes from the result is extremely hard. Shor’s Algorithm changes that by making factorization efficient on a quantum computer.

    Grover’s Algorithm was interesting too. It’s like making the “needle in a haystack” search faster, which means brute-forcing things like AES keys becomes more practical.

    Put together, it’s easy to see why Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) has become such a big topic.

    Didn’t get time to go deep into either algorithm today, just enough to understand the gist. But it’s fascinating how quantum computing changes what we consider “hard” problems compared to classical computing.

    Worth spending more time on this in the future.
